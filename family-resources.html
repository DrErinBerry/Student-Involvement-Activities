Student Involvement in the IEP — Oklahoma State Department of Education

These resources help families understand and support both transition planning and student involvement in the IEP process. Some are broad introductions to transition planning, while others focus specifically on self-determination and student involvement — each entry notes what it is best for and when to share it, so you can match the right resource to the right family at the right moment. A few resources are drawn from the original course materials and are noted accordingly. Bookmark this page as a quick reference!

Resource 1: Parent Center Hub — Students Get Involved

Best for: Parents new to student involvement in transition planning Website • Available in English and Spanish
Key Topics
  • Why and how students get involved in their own transition planning
  • Person-centered planning tools for families
  • Materials and connections made just for students themselves
  • Activities families can do at home to support self-determination
When to share: With parents who want to understand and encourage their child’s active role in transition planning, or who are preparing for their child’s first transition-focused IEP meeting.

Resource 2: PACER National Parent Center

Best for: Parents of middle/high school students wanting structured learning Online self-paced learning modules
Key Topics
  • Understanding IDEA and IEPs
  • Supporting the IEP team as a family member
  • College planning for students with disabilities
  • Employment preparation and work-based learning
  • Key differences between high school and adult services
When to share: With parents who prefer structured, self-paced learning and want to develop a deep understanding of the full transition process at their own pace.

Resource 3: Parent Guide to Transition Planning (PDF)

Best for: Parents who prefer printable reference materials Downloadable PDF
Key Topics
  • Four domains of transition: education, employment, community living, and independent living
  • Common parent concerns addressed in plain language
  • Parent roles as advocate, mentor, and life coach
  • Questions to ask at IEP meetings
When to share: Email before an IEP meeting so parents can review and bring questions, or provide printed copies at parent conferences and family nights.
Note: This guide is referenced in the Module 6 video as a general example of a parent transition guide. Some of the agencies and programs it lists are specific to California, where it was originally produced. Oklahoma families should use it for the general transition-planning concepts and self-advocacy ideas, and rely on the Oklahoma-specific resources for local agencies and services.

  • Understanding transition planning basics and timelines

Note: This NASET handout is referenced in the Module 6 video. Because it is an older publication, some of the links listed within the document may no longer work. Use it for the overview of transition topics it provides, and refer to the current, active resources on this page for working links.

Resource 5: I’m Determined Parent Video Series

Best for: All parents, especially those who prefer video over reading Short videos • Under 5 minutes each
Key Topics
  • Why student involvement matters and what it looks like at home
  • Supporting self-determination at home, at school, and in the community
  • How to partner effectively with schools on self-determination goals
  • Real families sharing their authentic experiences
When to share: Perfect for busy parents who can watch during a lunch break or while waiting for an appointment. Also ideal for parent nights, family workshops, and school newsletters. The short format removes the barrier of a long read.

Oklahoma Family Partner Resources & Self-Advocacy Programs

These Oklahoma-based organizations and programs offer direct support to families, along with self-advocacy and self-determination opportunities for students. They are excellent connections to share with families who want local help, peer support, or leadership experiences for their child.

Resource 6: Oklahoma Family Network (OFN)

Best for: Families who want one-to-one peer support and help finding services Statewide organization • Parent-to-parent support
Key Topics
  • Oklahoma’s parent-to-parent network for families of children and young adults with disabilities or special health care needs
  • Trained Support Parents who offer emotional support based on lived experience
  • Resource Navigators who help families find health care, disability, education, and job resources
When to share: With families who feel overwhelmed, are newly navigating the system, or simply want to talk with another parent who has been there.

Resource 7: Sooner SUCCESS

Best for: Families needing help coordinating services across agencies Statewide service navigation • Birth to age 21
Key Topics
  • Connects families of children with special needs to coordinated health, social, and educational services in their local community
  • Care navigation and coordination across systems
  • Regional coordinators who know local resources
When to share: With families who are juggling multiple agencies or services and need help pulling them together in their own community.

Resource 8: Oklahoma Parents Center (OPC)

Best for: Families who want to understand special education and become stronger advocates Statewide Parent Training & Information Center • Free • Birth to age 26
Key Topics
  • Understanding IDEA, Section 504, the IEP process, and transition
  • One-on-one support to prepare for and participate in IEP meetings
  • Free fact sheets, brochures, and trainings for parents and self-advocates
When to share: With families who have questions about their rights, want help preparing for a meeting, or are learning to advocate for their child or themselves. All OPC services are free to Oklahoma families.

Resource 9: DSACO — Boundaries and the Basics Toolkit

Best for: Families teaching personal safety, boundaries, and self-advocacy at home Downloadable toolkit • Modular series
Key Topics
  • Age-appropriate guidance for conversations about hygiene, relationships, and puberty
  • Personal safety, healthy boundaries, and consent
  • Builds a foundation for self-advocacy and self-care in adulthood
When to share: With families of students who are approaching or moving through adolescence and need supportive, concrete ways to discuss boundaries, safety, and growing independence. Developed by the Down Syndrome Association of Central Oklahoma, but useful for many families.

Resource 10: Oklahoma Youth Leadership Forum (YLF)

Best for: Families of high school students ready for a leadership and self-advocacy experience Week-long summer program • No cost to accepted students
Key Topics
  • High school students with disabilities spend a week on a college campus building leadership and advocacy skills
  • Students create a personal leadership plan, explore careers, and meet state legislators
  • A hands-on opportunity to practice self-determination and self-advocacy
When to share: With families of high school students (often juniors and seniors) who would benefit from a confidence-building, peer leadership experience. Funded by the Developmental Disabilities Council of Oklahoma; applications open annually.

Resource 11: Special Olympics Oklahoma

Best for: Families seeking community, belonging, and confidence-building for their child Year-round programs • Statewide
Key Topics
  • Year-round sports training and competition for children and adults with intellectual disabilities
  • Builds confidence, friendships, teamwork, and a sense of belonging
  • Leadership opportunities through programs such as Athlete Leadership
When to share: With families looking for community connection, physical activity, and opportunities for their child to build confidence and leadership outside the classroom.

Resource 12: Plan Your Way OK — Families

Best for: Families who want Oklahoma transition resources gathered in one place Website • Oklahoma-specific
Key Topics
  • Oklahoma’s central hub for transition resources, organized for families
  • Connections to Family Partners, state agencies, and community programs
  • Guidance on supporting self-advocacy and planning for life after high school
When to share: With any Oklahoma family beginning to think about transition — it gathers community programs, state agencies, and helpful information in one place so families do not have to search alone.

Addressing Common Parent Concerns

Remember: Parents are partners, not obstacles. Most families want their children to be more independent — they just need guidance on how to help. Use these responses to open conversations, not close them.

“My child is too shy to participate.”

We start small — maybe just reading a prepared statement or sharing one strength. We practice beforehand in a comfortable setting, so there are no surprises. Shy students often bloom when given structure, preparation, and support rather than open-ended expectations.

“This seems like too much pressure on my child.”

We take a gradual approach and always start with what your child is comfortable doing. The goal is empowerment, not stress. Multiple supports are in place at every step, and we lead with strengths before introducing challenges.

“Will important information be missed if my child leads?”

All legal requirements will still be covered. Adults guide the process — students participate in it; they do not replace the team. Student involvement actually adds information by including the one perspective the team cannot get anywhere else: your child’s own voice about their life and goals.

“My child doesn’t understand their disability.”

That is exactly why we teach self-awareness! We start with strengths and build understanding gradually using age-appropriate language and activities. Knowing themselves — what they need, what they are good at, and what they want — helps students advocate more effectively for themselves both now and in adulthood.
